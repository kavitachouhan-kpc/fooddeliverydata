Too many options, too little energy left to choose

Three in four students say food delivery apps overwhelm them with choice. Here's what the survey shows about how they get stuck, when it happens, and the shortcuts they use to get out.

The overload is real

Most students don't pick quickly. Half spend 10 to 20 minutes scrolling, and one in five spends over 20, often longer than the delivery itself takes to arrive.

The choosing happens at the end of the day

Eight in ten orders come at dinner or late at night, after a full day of classes, assignments and small decisions. That is exactly when decision fatigue theory predicts people have the least capacity to weigh options carefully.

How students cut through the noise

Faced with too much choice, people lean on mental shortcuts. The survey's findings read like a list of them. When trying a new restaurant or dish, students rank their deciding factors in this order:

  1. Recommendations from othersOutsourcing the decision to someone they trust.
  2. Good discountsA price signal that narrows the field instantly.
  3. Customer reviewsCrowd judgment as a stand-in for their own research.
  4. Social media posts and reelsA craving arrives pre-decided before the app is even open.

Other shortcuts in the data

  • Pictures over menus77% say food photos in the app shape what they order.
  • Cravings set by video59% say reels and shorts trigger cravings.
  • Sticking to the familiarFast food and Indian top the list: safe, known categories that need little thought.
  • A default order size54% order exactly two items, a stable habit rather than a fresh calculation each time.

What tired decisions cost

When willpower is low, the easier choice usually wins: the indulgent one, the one with extra items, the one that skips the healthy option.

80%think the apps make it easy to overspend
57%are no more likely to pick healthier food via apps than when dining out
53%see ordering in as a treat, plus 31% who say "sometimes"
21%usually spend more than ₹500 per order

The paper cites Capito & Pergelova (2023), who found people justify app orders partly through "food fatigue": being tired of deciding what to eat and cooking it. Ordering in can itself be a way to escape a decision.

Designing for a tired chooser

If overload is the problem, fewer and better-framed choices are the fix. These ideas follow from the survey's findings; none were tested in the study.

Make reordering effortless

Put last week's go-to order front and centre at dinner time, so the default choice takes one tap.

Surface friends' picks

Recommendations rank first, so show what classmates or people nearby order rather than another endless list.

Offer short, curated lists

Five strong options under ₹300 beats five hundred restaurants. A late-night mode with fewer, faster choices fits when most ordering happens.

Lead with photos

Visuals already drive decisions for 77% of students. A clear photo shortens the path to a choice more than a long description.

What the study can't tell us yet

The paper doesn't use the term decision fatigue or measure it directly. Feeling overwhelmed by many options is closer to choice overload; decision fatigue is about mental depletion from making many decisions in a row. The survey hints at both but separates neither. Research on willpower depletion has also had mixed replication results, so the theory itself is debated.

Questions a follow-up study could ask

  • Does feeling overwhelmed go with longer browsing or higher spend?The paper reports each separately but not how they relate.
  • Do evening orders take longer to decide than lunch orders?This would test the end-of-day pattern directly.
  • How often do students give up without ordering?Abandoned sessions are the clearest sign of overload.
  • How often do they reorder the same thing?Repeat orders show how much people rely on defaults.
  • Does using more apps make the overwhelm worse?With half on two or more apps, the choice set may be larger than any single app suggests.
